\chapter{From Neutron Stars to Kilonovae}\label{chap:BNMS}

Kilonovae are the main topic of study throughout this thesis.
Every chapter and section was built with the objective of developing the intuition on all the processes that take place in kilonovae.
However, the necessary astrophysical context will enrich our understanding.
Therefore, this chapter will first present the neutron star and its dense matter in the general relativity description. 
Then the text will move to the problem of finding two neutron stars in a close enough orbit and present a brief discussion of why the existence of such configurations is not trivial.
After that, a description of the different phases of the merger will be presented.
The different emission mechanisms will be discussed: gravitational waves, short gamma ray bursts and our favorite the kilonova.

\section{The Neutron Star}\label{NS}

Neutron stars contain the densest matter known to exist in a stable state. 
Their central densities reach several times the nuclear saturation density $n_{\rm sat}\simeq 0.15~\mathrm{fm^{-3}}$~\cite{horowitz_insights_2020}. 
In the outer layers of the star, nuclei coexist with a degenerate electron gas. 
With increasing density, electron captures make the nuclei progressively more neutron-rich until, at the neutron-drip density $n_{\rm drip}\simeq 2.5\times10^{-4}~\mathrm{fm^{-3}}$ ($\rho\simeq 4\times10^{11}~\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$), neutrons are no longer bound to the nuclei and form a free neutron gas~\cite{baym_neutron_1971}.
At about half the saturation density, the distinction between the inside and the outside of a nucleus effectively vanishes and matter becomes a continuum of nucleons, which marks the transition from crust to core. 
At the same time, the $\beta$ equilibrium favors neutrons instead of protons, which leads to a neutron dominated composition. 

Observed neutron-star masses range from about $1.1$ to slightly above $2\,M_\odot$, and their radii are inferred to lie between roughly $10$ and $14~\mathrm{km}$~\cite{ozel_masses_2016,riley_nicer_2021}.
However, a free degenerate neutron gas can support stars of at most $\simeq 0.7\,M_\odot$ \cite{oppenheimer_massive_1939,tolman_static_1939}.
To explain the observations accurately, the strongly repulsive short-range part of the nuclear interaction is required. 
The equation of state (EOS), therefore, has to include nuclear interaction terms, which is a formidable task to calculate.

The microscopic theory of this matter, quantum chromodynamics (QCD), cannot at present provide the EOS in the relevant density range.
At the baryon chemical potentials of neutron-star cores, QCD is non-perturbative, and lattice QCD is hampered by the fermion sign problem at finite baryon density. 
Controlled calculations exist only in two limits: chiral effective field theory describes nuclear matter up to about $1$--$2\,n_{\rm sat}$ \cite{hebeler_equation_2013}, and perturbative QCD becomes reliable only at densities of order $40\,n_{\rm sat}$, far above those realized in neutron stars \cite{kurkela_constraining_2014}.
In the intermediate regime, where neutron-star cores reside, the EOS is described by phenomenological models.
As a consequence, a first principles description of neutron star cores is still lacking.
Matter at several times $n_{\rm sat}$ may consist of nucleons, may contain hyperons, or may undergo a transition to deconfined quark matter, either through a smooth crossover or through a first order hadron-quark phase transition \cite{baym_hadrons_2018}.

Let us assume, for instance, that we know the EOS.
We can then estimate the radius of the star for a certain range of masses, including GR effects, through the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation:

\begin{equation}
  \frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{G}{r^2}\left ( \rho + \frac{P}{c^2}\right )\left ( m + 4\pi r^3 \frac{P}{c^2} \right )\left ( 1- \frac{2Gm}{c^2 r} \right )^{-1}.
\end{equation}

The equation assumes a static, spherically symmetric perfect fluid.
This is a good approximation, but real stars have angular momentum, so rotational corrections will be relevant. 
Here $\rho (r)$ and $P(r)$ are the density and pressure at a radial distance $r$. 
The quantity $m(r)$ is the mass within a sphere of radius $r$.
At this point we have not introduced any EOS yet.
The relationship between $P$ and $\rho$ could depend on temperature and composition.
Many current EOS models actually present their results for a range of temperatures and electron fractions $Y_e$.
Once the EOS is established, one can find a mass--radius curve. 
By comparing with observations one can in principle infer constraints on the EOS.
Such studies have been done, for example the existence of pulsars with masses of about $2\, M_\odot$~\cite{fonseca_refined_2021} and the radii inferred by NICER~\cite{riley_nicer_2021} constrain the pressure of dense matter up to densities of roughly $2-3\,n_{\rm sat}$ \cite{raaijmakers_constraints_2021}.
These measurements, however, constrain the relation between pressure and energy density rather than giving a full description of the matter. 
For example, hybrid stars (neutron stars with quark cores) can have mass--radius relations nearly indistinguishable from those of purely hadronic stars~\cite{alford_hybrid_2005}.

\section{Formation of binary neutron stars}\label{sec:formation}

For two neutron stars to merge, three conditions must be met.
Both stars must form, which requires two core-collapse supernovae (SNe), and the binary must survive both explosions.
Then the two remnants must end up close enough for gravitational-wave (GW) emission to bring them into contact in less than the age of the universe.
For typical neutron-star masses, the initial separation must be smaller than about $5\,R_\odot$.
This is two to three orders of magnitude smaller than the radii that the progenitor stars reach as supergiants.
The central problem of binary-neutron-star formation is therefore how to bring the compact remnants of two massive stars to separations far smaller than the stars themselves once were, without unbinding the system on the way.

In the standard isolated channel, a double neutron star (DNS) descends from a binary of two OB stars that are massive enough to end their lives in a core-collapse SN (see Fig.~\ref{fig:DNS_formation}).
If the stars of the initial binary are too far apart, the resulting DNS will not be close enough to merge.
If, on the contrary, the initial stars are close enough, they will interact via stable or unstable mass transfer. 
Once one of the stars reaches the end of its life, it expands and transfers part of its mass to its companion.
This star then explodes in a SN and leaves a NS, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:DNS_formation}. 
If the two stars remain bound after the supernova, they become a high-mass X-ray binary (HMXB).
When the second star expands, it engulfs the first star in a phase called common envelope \cite{paczynski_common_1976}.
In that stage, the dynamical friction on the first star as it moves inside the envelope of the second one causes the loss of angular momentum needed to bring the cores closer.
This phase is not stable, so eventually the envelope either falls onto the stars or is ejected, leaving behind the first NS and the He core of the second star, which eventually dies in what is called an ultra-stripped SN \cite{tauris_ultra-stripped_2015}. 
Finally, the system results in a DNS close enough to merge.
The sequence of events is reviewed in \cite{tauris_formation_2017}.

\section{Gravitational-wave-driven inspiral and the maximum separation}\label{sec:formation-gw}
 
Once both neutron stars have formed, the only process that changes the orbit appreciably is the emission of gravitational radiation.
Here we will show an estimate of the time to merger at leading (quadrupole) order and the maximum initial separation that allows a merger within the age of the Universe.
This calculation is based on the work presented in \cite{peters_gravitational_1963}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%  begin figure  %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Figures/binary.pdf}    
    \caption{Two neutron stars with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$  with separation $a$ seen from the center of mass frame.}
    \label{fig:binary}
\end{figure}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%  end figure  %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\noindent Consider two point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ on a circular orbit of separation $a$ and angular frequency $\omega=\sqrt{GM/a^3}$, with total mass $M = m_1 + m_2$ and reduced mass $\mu = m_1 m_2 / M$.
Their position vectors are $\mathbf{r}_1$ and $\mathbf{r}_2$, respectively ( see Fig.~\ref{fig:binary}).
Define the quadrupole mass tensor as

\begin{equation}
  Q_{ij} = \sum_l m_l r_{li}r_{lj},
\end{equation}

\noindent summing over the masses $m_l$ with $r_{li}=\textbf{r}_l\cdot \hat{\imath}$.
From this configuration, the positions are 
%\texttt{\textbackslash cos}
%\texttt{\textbackslash sin} 
\begin{equation}
  \textbf{r}_1=-\frac{\mu a}{m_1} \hat{n}\,;\quad \textbf{r}_2= \frac{\mu a}{m_2}\hat{n}\quad\text{with}\,\quad\hat{n}=(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta),0).
\end{equation} 


\noindent So the quadrupole mass tensor components are:
\begin{align}
  Q_{xx} &= \frac{\mu^2 a^2}{m_1}\cos^2(\theta)+\frac{\mu^2 a^2}{m_2}\cos^2(\theta)=\mu a^2 \cos^2(\theta)=\frac{\mu a^2}{2}(1+\cos(2\theta))\\
  Q_{yy} &= \mu a^2 \sin^2(\theta)=\frac{\mu a^2}{2}(1-\cos(2\theta))\\
  Q_{xy} &= \mu a^2 \sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)=\frac{\mu a^2}{2}(\sin(2\theta))
\end{align} 
Fortunately, the power radiated in the form of GWs ($L_{\rm GW}$) can be estimated using Eq.~(6) of Ref.~\cite{peters_gravitational_1963}.

\begin{align}
  L_{\rm GW} &= \frac{G}{5c^{5}}\left ( \dddot{Q}_{ij}\dddot{Q}_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\dddot{Q}_{ii}\dddot{Q}_{jj}\right )\\
  L_{\rm GW} &= \frac{G}{5c^{5}}\left ( {\dddot{Q}_{xx}}^2 + {\dddot{Q}_{yy}}^2+2{\dddot{Q}_{xy}}^2 -\frac{1}{3}(\dddot{Q}_{xx} + \dddot{Q}_{yy} )^2 \right ).
\end{align}
Since $\dddot Q_{xx}=-\dddot Q_{yy}$ the last term vanishes and with $\dddot{Q}_{xx}^{2} + \dddot{Q}_{yy}^{2} + 2\dddot{Q}_{xy}^{2}
= 32\,\mu^{2} a^{4}\omega^{6}$ (see Appendix \ref{ap:L_GW}), the GW luminosity is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lgw}
  L_{\rm GW} = \frac{32}{5}\frac{G}{c^{5}}\mu^{2}a^{4}\omega^{6}
             = \frac{32}{5}\frac{G^{4}}{c^{5}}\frac{(m_1 m_2)^{2} M}{a^{5}} .
\end{equation}
The energy radiated is drawn from the orbital energy $E = -G m_1 m_2/(2a)$. Setting $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}t = -L_{\rm GW}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dadt}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}a}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{\beta}{a^{3}}, \qquad
  \beta \equiv \frac{64}{5}\frac{G^{3} m_1 m_2 M}{c^{5}} .
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:dadt} integrates to $a^{4}(t) = a_0^{4} - 4\beta t$, so a binary with initial separation $a_0$ merges ($a \to 0$) after
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tc}
  t_m(a_0) = \frac{a_0^{4}}{4\beta}
  = \frac{5}{256}\frac{c^{5} a_0^{4}}{G^{3} m_1 m_2 M} .
\end{equation}
The steep $a_0^{4}$ dependence is the key point. 
Halving the separation shortens the inspiral by a factor of sixteen.

Now let us estimate the maximum separation for BNS to merge within the age of the Universe.
Note that, since the stars require a prior formation and evolution, this calculation overestimates the maximum distances between stars.
Let us require $t_{\rm m} \le t_{\rm U}$, with $t_{\rm U} \simeq 13.8~\mathrm{Gyr}$ the age of the Universe
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amax}
  a_{\max} = \left(4\beta\, t_{\rm U}\right)^{1/4}
  = \left(\frac{256}{5}\frac{G^{3} m_1 m_2 M}{c^{5}}\, t_{\rm U}\right)^{1/4} .
\end{equation} 
For $m_1 = m_2 = 1.35\,M_\odot$, $m_1 m_2 M = 4.92\,M_\odot^{3}$ and $\beta = 6.08\times10^{27}~\mathrm{cm^{4}\,s^{-1}}$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amax-num}
  a_{\max} \simeq 3.2\times10^{6}~\mathrm{km}\simeq 4.6\,R_\odot ,
\end{equation}
about $0.02~\mathrm{AU}$. As reference, red supergiants have typical radii of hundreds of $R_\odot$, so having neutron stars closer than $4.6\,R_\odot$ is quite a challenge.

\section{Mass ejection: tidal, shock-driven, and disk-wind ejecta}

At the last instants of the inspiral phase, when the separation becomes comparable to a few stellar radii, the point mass approximation is no longer accurate.
From those last instants to the first ones after the merger the system becomes really complex to study.
Physical phenomena and processes such as deformation, dense matter compression and heating, neutrino interactions and the appearance of strong magnetic fields become relevant.
Even though current merger simulations include specialized treatment for each of the previously mentioned effects there remain loopholes in our understanding~\cite{radice_dynamical_2016, radice_binary_2018}.
From the point of view of nucleosynthesis, the important outcome of the merger is the matter that becomes gravitationally unbound.
This matter is what we will call the ejecta and is divided into two categories according to the mechanism of ejection~\cite{metzger_kilonovae_2017}.
The first is the dynamical ejecta, it is expelled within $\sim10~\rm{ms}$ of the merger by tidal torques (tidal ejecta) and by shocks at the collision interface (shock-heated ejecta).
The second is the secular or post-merger ejecta, it is expelled after the dynamical ejecta by winds from the remnant and from the accretion disk that surrounds it.

\subsection{Dynamical ejecta}

The study of dynamical ejecta is closely related to numerical relativity simulations. 
Results from such simulations help us to estimate really important properties of the ejected material such as masses, velocities, distribution, temperatures and electron fraction depending on the sophistication of the simulation.
Current results from simulations estimate that the amount of dynamical ejecta lies between $\sim10^{-4}$ and $\sim10^{-2}M_\odot$, with asymptotic velocities of $0.1$--$0.3\,c$~\cite{radice_dynamics_2020}.
Electron fraction and distribution depend on the exact mechanism.
Tidal ejecta usually have low electron fraction ($Y_e<0.15$) and are ejected  mostly through the equator.
Shock-driven ejecta on the other hand could reach higher electron fractions ($Y_e<0.4$) and could be emitted at angles close to the poles (see Fig.~\ref{fig:remnant}).
One interesting result is that the channels are not equally likely to dominate.
The tidal component dominates for unequal masses and stiff EOS while the shock component dominates for equal masses and soft EOS. 
In addition, the total ejecta mass correlates with the tidal deformability and with the mass ratio and there are fits calibrated on simulations for such quantities~\cite{kruger_estimates_2020}.


\subsection{Post-merger ejecta}

Post-merger ejecta is quite interesting since its mechanism relies on the presence of winds from the remnant.
%The reader now may notice that after all this context we have not mention what is the remnant and it is a completely fair question to ask.
So, let us talk a little bit about the remnant. 
BNSMs have two possible outcomes: they become a black hole or another neutron star. 
The first outcome happens when there is a collapse and the interesting fact about this is that it could happen at any time during the merger. 
For example, the binary could become a very heavy unstable neutron star (long-lived remnant) that, after it cools down (typically seconds), collapses to a black hole.
The collapse can happen also at the moment of the merger (prompt collapse) or during a viscous phase shortly after (short-lived remnant).

In all but the most prompt collapses, part of the material with high angular momentum settles into a rotationally supported torus around the central object, with masses $\sim10^{-3}$--$0.2M_\odot$.
At early times the disk is dense, hot and has low electron fraction.
As the disk spreads the viscous heating and the synthesis of $\alpha$ particles and heavier nuclei drive the outflow~\cite{shibata_merger_2019}.
As a result part of the disk accumulates enough kinetic energy to unbind from the remnant contributing to the kilonova.

\section{Weak interactions and the electron fraction of the ejecta}

Before the merger, the stars can be described as cold neutron rich matter.
At the merger, as we mentioned, the matter heats up and several effects take place.
As the matter moves from the $\beta$ equilibrium, neutrinos become important since they control the $Y_e$ distribution across the ejecta.
This is why neutrino transport schemes are important in merger simulations.
Simulations without them mispredict the $Y_e$ and are not useful for abundance calculations. 
This section will establish first why $Y_e$ is important and then how neutrinos control it. 

The outcome of the r-process in an expanding fluid element is set mainly by three quantities evaluated when nuclear statistical equilibrium freezes out: the electron fraction $Y_e$, the entropy per baryon $s$, and the expansion timescale $\tau_{\rm exp}$~\cite{lippuner_r-process_2015}.
They fix the neutron-to-seed ratio at the onset of neutron captures.
Merger ejecta have moderate entropies, $s\sim10$--$30\,k_B$ per baryon, and short expansion times, $\tau_{\rm exp}\sim$~ms, so $Y_e$ is the dominant parameter.
By charge neutrality,
\begin{equation}
  Y_e = \frac{n_{e^-}-n_{e^+}}{n_b} = \sum_i Z_iY_i,\qquad Y_i=\frac{X_i}{A_i},
\end{equation}
where $X_i$ is the mass fraction ($\rho_i/\rho$), $A_i$ and $Z_i$ are the mass number and proton number of species $i$ respectively and $n_{e^\pm},n_b$ are number densities of electrons, positrons and baryons.
Strong and electromagnetic reactions conserve $Y_e$.
It changes only through charged-current weak interactions.

In a cold neutron star the neutrinos escape freely ($\mu_\nu=0$) and matter is in neutrinoless $\beta$ equilibrium, $\mu_n=\mu_p+\mu_e$\footnote{Here $\mu$ refers to chemical potentials}. 
Because $\mu_e$ grows rapidly with density, equilibrium favors neutrons.
In the inner crust and outer core, from which the dynamical ejecta originate, $Y_e$ has values just above the neutron drip to $\approx0.03$--$0.1$ at $\rho\sim10^{13}$--$10^{14}~\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.
If this matter were ejected without further weak processing, it would have a neutron-to-seed ratio of several hundred and produce a robust abundance pattern from the second peak up to the actinides~\cite{korobkin_astrophysical_2012}.
During and after the merger, however, the ejecta are heated and irradiated, and two classes of reactions can change $Y_e$:
\begin{align}
  e^-+p &\rightleftarrows n+\nu_e, & e^++n &\rightleftarrows p+\bar\nu_e,\label{eq:capt}\\
  \nu_e+n &\rightarrow p+e^-, & \bar\nu_e+p &\rightarrow n+e^+.\label{eq:abs}
\end{align}
Captures on heavy nuclei and the inverse reactions with thermal neutrinos also contribute in dense, opaque regions, but for the ejecta the reactions on free nucleons dominate.

For matter composed of free nucleons the abundances are $Y_p=Y_e$ and $Y_n=1-Y_e$, and the reactions~\ref{eq:capt}--\ref{eq:abs} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dYedt}
  \frac{dY_e}{dt} = \underbrace{\left(\lambda_{\nu_en}+\lambda_{e^+n}\right)}_{\equiv\lambda_+}(1-Y_e) - \underbrace{\left(\lambda_{\bar\nu_ep}+\lambda_{e^-p}\right)}_{\equiv\lambda_-}Y_e .
\end{equation}
Where $\lambda_{ab}$ is the rate of the reaction reactants $ab$. 
For slowly varying rates, $Y_e$ relaxes exponentially to the instantaneous equilibrium value
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Yeeq}
  Y_e^{\rm eq}=\frac{\lambda_+}{\lambda_++\lambda_-}
  \quad\text{on the timescale}\quad
  \tau_{\rm weak}=\frac{1}{\lambda_++\lambda_-}.
\end{equation}
Whether the ejecta reach equilibrium is decided by the ratio $\tau_{\rm exp}/\tau_{\rm weak}$.
If $\tau_{\rm exp}\gg\tau_{\rm weak}$, $Y_e$ follows $Y_e^{\rm eq}$; if $\tau_{\rm exp}\ll\tau_{\rm weak}$, $Y_e$ is frozen.
Because both the temperature and the neutrino flux fall quickly as the matter expands, every fluid element eventually freezes out.
This is why the slow expanding post-merger ejecta reach higher $Y_e$ than the rapidly expanding tidal ejecta, which are cold and fast and keep $Y_e$ similar to the interior of the star before the merger.

\section{Electromagnetic counterparts}

GW170817 showed that a BNS merger is accompanied by emission across the whole electromagnetic spectrum~\cite{abbott_multi-messenger_2017}.
The gamma-ray burst GRB~170817A was detected $1.7~\mathrm{s}$ after the merger~\cite{abbott_gravitational_2017}.
The optical counterpart AT2017gfo was found $10.9~\mathrm{h}$ later in the galaxy NGC~4993 at $\simeq40~\mathrm{Mpc}$~\cite{coulter_swope_2017}.
In addition, an X-ray and radio afterglow appeared days later and kept brightening for several months.
At this point we have briefly discussed the formation of the binary, the GW emission, the mass ejection channels and the initial conditions of the ejected material. 
Now it is time to talk about electromagnetic emissions.

After the merger (seconds) the first emission generated is the gamma ray burst from a relativistic jet, which corresponds to a non thermal radiation.
As the density decreases and the ejecta approaches an optically thin regime, thermalized photons escape and build the kilonova (days).
Also, some non thermal afterglows are produced when the jet and the ejecta decelerate (hours).

\subsection{GRBs}

Gamma ray bursts (GRBs) are high energy photons released from the polar regions by ultra-relativistic, collimated outflows called jets launched by the remnant. This could be an accreting black hole, which extracts its energy through magnetic fields~\cite{blandford_electromagnetic_1977}, or a rapidly rotating magnetized neutron star.
The photons are produced from the acceleration of the charges inside the jet.
When the jet drives into the surrounding medium it decelerates and produces a non thermal afterglow.

However, GRBs are not unique to mergers.
Some GRBs are associated with the deaths of massive stars.
In the collapsar model the core of the rapidly rotating, stripped massive star collapses to a black hole surrounded by a massive accretion disk, which powers a jet for tens of seconds~\cite{woosley_gamma-ray_1993,macfadyen_collapsars_1999}. 
The working assumption was therefore that the origin of the GRBs can be identified by the duration.
Short GRBs come from mergers and long GRBs from collapsars.
But in recent years this was proved wrong.
GRB 230307A had a prompt phase of tens of seconds followed by a red kilonova~\cite{levan_heavy-element_2024,yang_lanthanide-rich_2024}, and GRB 200826A had a duration of $0.65~\mathrm{s}$ and was followed by a supernova~\cite{ahumada_discovery_2021}.
The duration of a burst is therefore not a reliable indicator of its progenitor.
Now the central open question is how a merger can power a jet for tens of seconds, when the accretion time of a typical post-merger disk is  $\lessapprox1~\mathrm{s}$.

\subsection{Kilonovae}

Kilonovae are thermal emission since their origin comes from the thermalization of the energy from radioactive decays.
However, its emission is not exactly the one of a black body, it could have deviations, so a kilonova is a quasi-thermal transient.
Most kilonova models, from analytic like LP98 to multidimensional radiative transfer, are built from the same few ingredients: homologous expansion of the ejecta, a radioactive heating rate and its thermalization efficiency, and opacity set by the composition and photon diffusion out of an expanding medium.

\subsubsection{Homologous expansion}
 
At early stages the dynamical ejecta is accelerated but shortly after the pressure forces become negligible and every fluid element moves ballistically, $r=vt$. One model for the density that encapsulates this behavior is the one presented in Ref.~\cite{lippuner_r-process_2015}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:homol}
  \rho(\tau_{\rm exp},t)=
  \left \{
    \begin{array}{lr}
      \rho_0e^{-t/\tau_{\rm exp}} & \text{if}\, t\leq 3\tau_{\rm exp} \\
      \rho_0 \left (\frac{3\tau_{\rm exp}}{et}\right )^3& \text{if}\, t \geq 3\tau_{\rm exp} \\
    \end{array}
  \right \}.
\end{equation}
where $\rho_0$ is an initial density and $\tau_{\rm exp}$ the expansion time. 
Notice that this model represents a smooth transition from an exponential expansion to a ballistic expansion. 


\subsubsection{Radioactive heating}

As nuclei decay part of their energy stored as mass is released in the form of kinetic energy and photons.
Part of the kinetic energy escapes from the system in form of neutrinos and the rest thermalizes through plasma interactions.
So effectively not all the energy from decays is used to power the transient.
This subsection focuses on the energy and the next subsection will talk about thermalization.
Let us first define the nuclear energy released per unit mass from decays, $\dot\epsilon$,  as 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dotepsilon}
  \dot \epsilon(t)=\sum_{i,x} \frac{Q_{i,x}}{M_{ej}}\lambda_{i,x}N_i(t)= \sum_{i,x} \frac{Q_{i,x}}{m_i}\lambda_{i,x}X_i(t)
\end{equation}
where the index $i$ represents the sum over all the nuclear species and the index $x$ represents the sum over decay modes. 
$Q_{i,x}$ is the energy released by species $i$ that decay through the decay mode $x$.
$\lambda_{i,x}$ is the decay constant through the decay mode $x$ of species $i$.
$X_i$ is the mass fraction and $m_i$ is the mass of a nucleus $i$.
The alternative version uses $M_{ej}$ (the mass of the ejecta) and $N_i$ the total number of nuclei of species $i$.
At this point this equation does not account for thermalization effects.
This definition is completely consistent with the standard definition of heat per baryon due to changes in the composition.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dotheat}
  \dot q =-\sum_i (\mu_i+Z_i\mu_e)\dot Y_i
\end{equation} 
Where $Y_i$ is the number fraction defined as $n_i/n$ which is the number density of $i$ over the number density of baryons, $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential of species $i$. 
Notice that Eq.~\ref{eq:dotepsilon} only accounts for decays while the Eq.~\ref{eq:dotheat} accounts for any changes in the compositions (decay and reactions).
They are consistent in the sense that if we restrict Eq.~\ref{eq:dotheat} to account for changes only through decays, that is, by establishing an expression for $\dot Y_i$, we get Eq.~\ref{eq:dotepsilon}.
Now the issue becomes how to correctly estimate the abundances ($X_i$,$Y_i$) as a function of time while taking into account all reactions and decays involved. 
That problem is solved by implementing a nuclear reaction network which we will treat in detail in Chapter~\ref{ch:Nuclear_reaction_net}.
So kilonova models usually implement a reaction network or at least some fits like power laws.
 
The power-law behavior of $\dot q$ can be understood with a statistical argument~\cite{metzger_electromagnetic_2010,hotokezaka_analytic_2017}.
Due to the large number of species Eq.~\ref{eq:dotepsilon} can be approximated as an integral.
From Fermi's theory of allowed $\beta$ decay the rate scales as, $\lambda\propto Q^{5}$.
Also, $Q\propto\eta$ with $\eta=N-Z$ (neutron excess).
Assuming that number of nuclei per neutron excess interval is constant $dN\propto d\eta$\footnote{The N presented here represents number of nuclei not neutrons} it is easy to see that
\begin{equation}
  dN=N_0d\eta=N_0\lambda^{-4/5}d\lambda,
\end{equation} 
\noindent where $N_0$ is a normalization constant.
Let us now calculate the heating rate as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \dot\epsilon=\sum_i \frac{Q_i\lambda_i}{M_{ej}} N_i(t)\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{M_{ej}}\lambda^{1/5}\lambda N_0e^{-\lambda t}\lambda^{-4/5}d\lambda
\end{equation} 
\begin{equation}
  \dot \epsilon \propto t^{-7/5}
\end{equation}

Network calculations confirm this behavior, $\dot \epsilon\simeq10^{10}~\mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\,(t/\mathrm{day})^{-1.3}$~\cite{metzger_electromagnetic_2010,korobkin_astrophysical_2012}.
A widely used fit is~\cite{korobkin_astrophysical_2012}
\begin{align}\label{eq:korobkin}
  \dot \epsilon(t)=\dot \epsilon_0\left[\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{t-t_0}{\sigma}\right)\right]^{\alpha},\\
  \quad \dot \epsilon_0=2\times10^{18}~\mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}},\; t_0=1.3~\mathrm{s},\; \sigma=0.11~\mathrm{s},\; \alpha=1.3,
\end{align}
which reproduces the plateau during the r-process and the subsequent power law.
 
\subsubsection{Thermalization}
 
Only the fraction of the decay energy that is deposited in the ejecta can be radiated.
The effective heating rate is $\dot \varepsilon(t)=\sum_x f_{\rm x}(t)\,\dot \epsilon_x(t)$, with $\dot\epsilon_x(t)=\sum_i\dot \epsilon_{i,x}(t)$ and $f_{\rm x}$ the thermalization function of decay mode $x$. 
Since thermalization depends on the nature of the decay products then $f_{x}(t)=\sum_p \chi_p\,f_p(t)$ where $\chi_p$ is the average fraction of the energy carried by the product $p$ and $p$ can be $\gamma,\alpha,\beta$ or fission daughters. The $f_p$ function is the specific thermalization for the product $p$.
Gamma rays deposit their energy if the ejecta are optically thick to them.
A convenient interpolation is $f_\gamma\simeq1-\exp(-t_\gamma^{2}/t^{2})$ where $t_\gamma$ is a function of the mass, velocity and opacity of the ejecta~\cite{barnes_radioactivity_2016,hotokezaka_radioactive_2016}. 
Most of the gamma-ray energy therefore escapes after about a day, around the time of the peak.

Electrons, $\alpha$ particles and fission fragments are confined by magnetic fields and lose energy by ionization and excitation, at a rate proportional to the density.
A convenient way to approximate the thermalization is presented in Ref.~\cite{kasen_radioactive_2019},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:te}
  f_e(t)\simeq\left(1+\frac{t}{t_e}\right)^{-n},\qquad n\simeq1\text{--}1.5.
\end{equation}

Here $t_e$ is a timescale that depends on the dynamics and mass of the ejecta.
This formula can be applied to any charged particle by using different values for $n$.

\subsubsection{Opacity}
 
The free electrons provide only a small opacity, because the ejecta are cool and only weakly ionized.
The opacity of kilonova ejecta is instead dominated by the very large number of bound--bound transitions of the r-process elements which are largely unknown.
The first models and radiative transfer simulations used relatively low opacities assuming that the  optical properties of the ejecta matched the ones from ordinary SNe, mostly related to iron group lines.
Such assumptions were later proved wrong as observations matched a kilonova with a high opacity component.
Now, one may think that opacity is then related to the number of electrons an atom has but it is rather the large number of distinct ways of moving electrons within the open shells that increases the opacity. 
As reference, the number of ways of placing $n$ electrons in a shell with $g=2(2\ell+1)$ states is $\binom{g}{n}$, at most 252 for a $d$ shell ($g=10$) but 3432 for an $f$ shell ($g=14$).
The lanthanides and actinides, with open $4f$ and $5f$ shells, therefore have vastly more levels and lines than the iron group, and an opacity higher by one to two orders of magnitude, concentrated at optical and ultraviolet wavelengths~\cite{kasen_opacities_2013,tanaka_systematic_2020}.
In grey models this is captured by $\kappa\simeq0.5$--$1~\mathrm{cm^{2}\,g^{-1}}$ for lanthanide-poor ejecta ($Y_e\gtrsim0.3$) and $\kappa\simeq10$--$30~\mathrm{cm^{2}\,g^{-1}}$ for lanthanide-rich ejecta ($X_{\rm La}\gtrsim10^{-2}$, $Y_e\lesssim0.25$)\footnote{$X_{\rm La}$ is defined as the sum of the mass fractions of all lanthanides.}.
 
\subsubsection{Diffusion and the time of the peak}
 
Photons produced in the ejecta escape by diffusion.
For a uniform sphere the optical depth\footnote{This $\tau$ is different from the expansion time $\tau_{\rm exp}$ and here we are assuming that the ejecta is already in the ballistic regime (expanding with constant velocity).} is $\tau=\kappa\rho R=3\kappa M/(4\pi v^{2}t^{2})$, and the diffusion time (the time needed for the photons to reach the surface) is $t_d\simeq\tau R/c$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:td}
  t_d=\frac{3\kappa M_{ej}}{4\pi\,c\,v\,t}\equiv\frac{\tau_m^{2}}{t},\qquad
  \tau_m\equiv\left(\frac{3\kappa M_{ej}}{4\pi\,c\,v}\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
At early times $t_d\gg t$ and the radiation is trapped and degraded by adiabatic expansion.
The emission peaks roughly when $t \simeq \tau_m$.
