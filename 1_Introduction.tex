\section{Dense matter and the neutron-star equation of state}\label{sec:intro-eos}

Neutron stars contain the densest matter known to exist in a stable state. 
Their central densities reach several times the nuclear saturation density $n_{\rm sat}\simeq 0.15~\mathrm{fm^{-3}}$~\cite{horowitz_insights_2020}. 
At such densities atoms are not stable configurations, and matter adopts a different form governed by the strong and weak interactions called dense matter.
In the outer layers of the star, nuclei coexist with a degenerate electron gas. 
With increasing density, electron captures make the nuclei progressively more neutron-rich until, at the neutron-drip density $n_{\rm drip}\simeq 2.5\times10^{-4}~\mathrm{fm^{-3}}$ ($\rho\simeq 4\times10^{11}~\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$), neutrons are no longer bound to the nuclei and form a free neutron gas~\cite{baym_neutron_1971}.
At about half the saturation density the difference between inside and outside of a nucleus vanish effectively becoming a continuum of nucleons which marks the transition from crust to core. 
At the same time the $\beta$ equilibrium favors neutrons instead of protons, this leads to a neutron dominated composition.  
The mechanism from which neutrons drip at such densities and why nature favors neutrons will be addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:mergers}.

Observed neutron-star masses range from about $1.1$ to slightly above $2\,M_\odot$, and their radii are inferred to lie between roughly $10$ and $14~\mathrm{km}$~\cite{ozel_masses_2016,riley_nicer_2021}.
Their structure is determined by the equation of state (EOS) through the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equations plus rotational corrections.
Since the matter inside the star is expected to be dominated by neutrons, the EOS will inherit the nature predicted from the quantum behavior and interactions of neutrons.
A free, degenerate neutron gas can support stars of at most $\simeq 0.7\,M_\odot$ \cite{oppenheimer_massive_1939}.
To accurate explain observations the strongly repulsive short-range part of the nuclear interaction is required, which in relativistic mean-field models is mediated by $\omega$-meson exchange. 
Through this link between microphysics and astrophysical observables, neutron stars have become natural laboratories for strongly interacting matter at densities that cannot be reached in terrestrial experiments.

The microscopic theory of this matter, quantum chromodynamics (QCD), cannot at present provide the EOS in the relevant density range.
At the baryon chemical potentials of neutron-star cores, QCD is non-perturbative, and lattice QCD is hampered by the fermion sign problem at finite baryon density. 
Controlled calculations exist only in two limits, chiral effective field theory describes nuclear matter up to about $1$--$2\,n_{\rm sat}$ \cite{hebeler_equation_2013}, and perturbative QCD becomes reliable only at densities of order $40\,n_{\rm sat}$, far above those realized in neutron stars \cite{kurkela_constraining_2014}.
In the intermediate regime, where neutron-star cores reside, the EOS is described by phenomenological models whose predictions must be confronted with astrophysical observations.

As a consequence, the composition of neutron star cores remains unknown.
Matter at several times $n_{\rm sat}$ may consist of nucleons, may contain hyperons, or may undergo a transition to deconfined quark matter, either through a smooth crossover or through a first order hadron-quark phase transition \cite{baym_hadrons_2018}. 
Heavy ion collisions probe hot matter with proton fractions close to those of stable nuclei ($Y_p\simeq 0.4$--$0.5$) and cannot access the cold, neutron rich, $\beta$-equilibrated regime of neutron stars.
Astrophysical observations therefore play a unique role in constraining this region of the QCD phase diagram.

\section{Binary neutron-star mergers as multimessenger laboratories}\label{sec:intro-mergers}

Observations of isolated neutron stars have placed significant constraints on the EOS. 
The existence of pulsars with masses of about $2\,M_\odot$~\cite{fonseca_refined_2021} and the radii inferred by NICER~\cite{riley_nicer_2021} constrain the pressure of dense matter up to densities of roughly $2-3\,n_{\rm sat}$ \cite{raaijmakers_constraints_2021}. 
These measurements, however, constrain the relation between pressure and energy density rather than giving a full description of the matter. 
For example, hybrid stars (Neutron stars with quark cores) can have mass-radius relations nearly indistinguishable from those of purely hadronic stars~\cite{alford_hybrid_2005}.

Binary neutron star mergers (BNSMs) provide complementary information.
During the inspiral, the tidal deformability of the stars imprints the phase of the gravitational-wave (GW) signal.
For GW170817, this effect constrained the binary tidal deformability $\tilde\Lambda$ to values of roughly $600$-$800$ depending on the waveform model and the priors \cite{de_tidal_2018}.
BNSMs also provide information about the nuclear physics phenomena that takes place during sudden expansion of the dense matter. 
Since neutron rich material ejected during the merger undergoes rapid neutron capture (r-process) nucleosynthesis, fresh unstable nuclei are been form.
Their radioactive decays powers a kilonova, a thermal optical and infrared transient, whose luminosity and colour is sensible to its composition through the radioactive heating rate and the
opacity~\cite{li_transient_1998,metzger_electromagnetic_2010,yang_lanthanide-rich_2024,hotokezaka_radioactive_2020}. 
The kilonova AT2017gfo, which followed GW170817, confirmed BNSMs as sites of the r-process \cite{abbott_gw170817_2017,kasen_origin_2017}.

BNSMs therefore connect the EOS of dense matter with the nuclear physics of heavy-element formation.
Extracting this information from the observed signals, however, is not straightforward, because each step from the microphysics to an observable introduces additional modeling uncertainties.
Current merger simulations have to face three major limitations.
First, an accurate treatment of neutrino transport. Neutrinos interaction changes the $Y_e$ distribution which impacts the r-process nucleosynthesis and therefore the kilonova.
Current merger simulations include neutrino treatment approximated by leakage, moment, or Monte Carlo transport
schemes~\cite{foucart_neutrino_2023}. 
Second, nucleosynthesis calculations require the properties of thousands of neutron-rich nuclei far from stability, most of which have never been measured and are taken from global theoretical models of nuclear masses, $\beta$-decay half-lives, and reaction rates~\cite{rauscher_astrophysical_2000}.
Finally, kilonova models require atomic opacities dominated by the bound-bound transitions of lanthanides and actinides, which are themselves only approximately known~\cite{tanaka_radiative_2013}.

Constraints on the EOS derived from the kilonova of GW170817 illustrate this fragility, as they were later shown to depend sensitively on modeling assumptions~\cite{radice_gw170817_2018,kiuchi_revisiting_2019}.
Also, no further neutron-star merger with an electromagnetic counterpart was detected, so the interpretation of future events will rely heavily on theoretical predictions.
In particular, any attempt to use kilonovae to probe the EOS, for instance to search for signatures of a hadron-quark phase transition, requires that the contribution of nuclear-physics uncertainties to the predicted light curves be quantified first.

\section{Nuclear-physics uncertainties in kilonova light curves}\label{sec:intro-nuclear}

The kilonova depends on the EOS only indirectly, through the mass, velocity, and composition of the ejecta.
The composition determines the opacity, material with electron fraction $Y_e\lesssim 0.25$ produces lanthanides, which strongly increase the opacity and shift the emission to longer timescales and redder
wavelengths~%\textbf{Kasen2013,Lippuner2015}. 
The radioactive heating
that powers the kilonova, in turn, depends on the decay properties of
nuclei far from stability, many of which have not been measured and
must be taken from theoretical models %~\textbf{Mumpower2016,Barnes2021}.

These two sources of uncertainty act on different physical
quantities but on the same observable. An EOS-induced change in the
ejecta mass and a change in the theoretical $\beta$-decay rates can
both alter the luminosity and timescale of the kilonova. Likewise, a
purely hadronic EOS with a tidal deformability similar to that of a
hybrid EOS produces a nearly identical inspiral. A central question is
therefore whether nuclear-physics uncertainties can mask the signature
of a phase transition in the electromagnetic signal, and whether the
combination with the GW signal can break this degeneracy.

\section{Objectives and research questions}


The objective of this thesis is to perform a proof-of-concept joint
analysis of the gravitational-wave and kilonova signals of a binary
neutron-star merger, in order to assess whether a hadron--quark phase
transition leaves signatures that can be distinguished from those of
hadronic EOS variations and from nuclear-physics uncertainties.
Specifically, we address the following questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How do the gravitational-wave signal and the dynamical
        ejecta (mass, velocity, and thermodynamic conditions) of an
        equal-mass merger differ between hadronic EOSs and an EOS
        with a first-order hadron--quark phase transition, and are
        these differences larger than the numerical uncertainty?
  \item How do the EOS-induced changes in the nucleosynthesis yields,
        radioactive heating, and kilonova light curves compare with
        those produced by different theoretical prescriptions for
        $\beta$-decay rates, and does the joint GW+EM analysis help
        disentangle the two?
\end{enumerate}

The study is restricted to a single equal-mass binary, three EOSs
[names], and the dynamical ejecta; the long-term disk outflows and
neutrinos as a direct observable are beyond its scope.
