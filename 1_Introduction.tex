\section{Dense matter and the neutron-star equation of state}
Neutron stars contain the densest matter known to exist in a stable state. 
Their central densities reach several times the nuclear saturation density $n_{\rm sat}\simeq 0.15~\mathrm{fm^{-3}}$ \cite{horowitz_insights_2020}. 
At densities reached in neutron stars atoms configurations are unlikely and matter adopts a different form. 
Physicists called this state of matter, in absent of better names, dense matter and has been the protagonist in the study of compact objects. 
To understand its composition an properties we should begin by the lightest tail of the dense matter.
Between densities of $n_{drip}\simeq 2.5\time 10^{-4}~\mathrm{fm^{-3}}$ to $n_{\rm sat}$ neutrons inside nuclei begin to unbound so there are nuclei, electrons and free neutrons.
When density reach saturation the difference between inside and outside of a nucleus vanish effectively becoming a continuum of nucleons \cite{baym_neutron_1971}. 
At the same time the $\beta$ equilibrium favors neutrons instead of protons, this leads to a neutron dominated composition.  
The mechanism from which neutrons drip at such densities and why nature favors neutrons will be addressed in chapter $2$.
 
Neutron stars masses ranges from $\simeq 1.1 - 2 M_\odot$ with radius from $\simeq 5-15\,\mathrm{km}$ \cite{ozel_masses_2016}.
Their structure is determined by the equation of state (EOS) of this matter through the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equations plus rotational corrections.
Since the matter inside the star is excepted to be dominated by neutrons, the EOS will inherit the nature predicted from the quantum behavior and interactions of neutrons.
Phenomena such as pauli exclusion principle manifest as the degeneracy pressure and repulsive interaction between neutrons through $\omega$ meson exchange adds the necessary pressure to prevent the collapse setting up a specific radius for a given mass and rotation.  
This link between microphysics to astrophysical observables opened a window to study of dense matter through compact object observations. 
Neutron stars are therefore natural laboratories for strongly interacting matter at densities that cannot be reached in terrestrial experiments.

However the microscopic theory of this matter, quantum chromodynamics (QCD), cannot at present provide the EOS in the relevant density range.
At the baryon chemical potentials of neutron-star cores, QCD is non-perturbative, and lattice QCD is hampered by the fermion sign problem at finite baryon density. 
Controlled calculations exist in the chiral effective field theory limit (up to 2 times nuclear saturation) \cite{hebeler_equation_2013}, and perturbative QCD becomes reliable only at densities of order $40\,n_{\rm sat}$, far above those realized in neutron stars \cite{kurkela_constraining_2014}.
In the intermediate regime, where neutron-star cores reside, the EOS is described by phenomenological models whose predictions must be confronted with astrophysical observations.

As a consequence, the composition of neutron star cores remains unknown.
Matter at several times $n_{\rm sat}$ may consist of nucleons, may contain hyperons, or may undergo a transition to deconfined quark matter, either through a smooth crossover or through a first order hadron-quark phase transition \cite{baym_hadrons_2018}. 
Heavy ion collisions probe hot and nearly symmetric ($Y_e\simeq0.5$) matter and cannot access the cold, neutron rich, $\beta$-equilibrated regime of neutron stars.
Astrophysical observations therefore play a unique role in constraining this region of the QCD phase diagram.

\section{Binary neutron-star mergers as multimessenger laboratories}

Observations of isolated neutron stars have placed significant constraints on the EOS. 
The existence of pulsars with masses of about $2\,M_\odot$ \cite{fonseca_refined_2021} and the radii inferred by NICER \cite{riley_nicer_2021} constrain the pressure of dense matter up to roughly $2-3\,n_{\rm sat}$. 
These quantities, however, constrain the pressure energy density relation rather than giving a full description of the matter. 
For example, hybrid stars (Neutron stars with quark cores) can have mass-radius relations nearly indistinguishable from those of purely hadronic stars \cite{alford_hybrid_2005}.

Binary neutron star mergers (BNSMs) provide complementary information.
During the inspiral, the tidal deformability of the stars imprints the gravitational wave (GW) phase.
This has been proved in the GW170817 to constrain the tidal deformability $\tilde\Lambda$ to values between  $80$ and $700$ \cite{de_tidal_2018}. 
After the merger, the remnant reaches higher densities and temperatures than any isolated neutron star, so the post-merger dynamics are sensitive to the EOS in a regime that isolated stars cannot probe. 
In addition, the neutron rich material ejected during the merger undergoes rapid neutron capture (r-process) nucleosynthesis, and the radioactive decay of the newly formed nuclei powers a kilonova. 
The kilonova AT2017gfo, which followed GW170817, confirmed BNSMs as a site of the r-process \cite{abbott_gw170817_2017,kasen_origin_2017}.

Extracting EOS information from these signals is, however, far from straightforward. Each step between the EOS and an observable introduces additional uncertainties: 
numerical resolution and the
treatment of neutrino interactions in merger simulations, the
properties of neutron-rich nuclei far from stability, and the atomic
opacities of the ejecta. Constraints on the EOS derived from the
kilonova of GW170817 illustrate this fragility, as they were later
shown to depend sensitively on modeling
assumptions~%\textbf{Bauswein2017,Radice2018Lambda,Kiuchi2019}.
Moreover, no further neutron-star merger with an electromagnetic
counterpart was detected during the fourth LIGO--Virgo--KAGRA
observing run, so the interpretation of future events will rely
heavily on theoretical predictions. Systematic studies of how
uncertainties propagate from the EOS and nuclear physics to the
observable signals are therefore required.

\section{Phase transitions and nuclear-physics uncertainties}


The observational signatures of a hadron--quark phase transition
depend on the density at which it occurs. If the onset density lies
below the central density of the inspiraling stars, quark cores are
already present before the merger and modify the stellar radii, the
tidal deformability, and the tidally ejected material. If the onset
is reached only in the post-merger remnant, the inspiral is unaffected
and the transition manifests itself only after the merger. In the
hybrid model considered in this work, [quark matter is already present
in the inspiraling stars], so the transition can affect both phases.

The kilonova depends on the EOS only indirectly, through the mass,
velocity, and composition of the ejecta. The composition determines
the opacity: material with electron fraction $Y_e\lesssim 0.25$
produces lanthanides, which strongly increase the opacity and shift
the emission to longer timescales and redder
wavelengths~%\textbf{Kasen2013,Lippuner2015}. 
The radioactive heating
that powers the kilonova, in turn, depends on the decay properties of
nuclei far from stability, many of which have not been measured and
must be taken from theoretical models %~\textbf{Mumpower2016,Barnes2021}.

These two sources of uncertainty act on different physical
quantities but on the same observable. An EOS-induced change in the
ejecta mass and a change in the theoretical $\beta$-decay rates can
both alter the luminosity and timescale of the kilonova. Likewise, a
purely hadronic EOS with a tidal deformability similar to that of a
hybrid EOS produces a nearly identical inspiral. A central question is
therefore whether nuclear-physics uncertainties can mask the signature
of a phase transition in the electromagnetic signal, and whether the
combination with the GW signal can break this degeneracy.

\section{Objectives and research questions}


The objective of this thesis is to perform a proof-of-concept joint
analysis of the gravitational-wave and kilonova signals of a binary
neutron-star merger, in order to assess whether a hadron--quark phase
transition leaves signatures that can be distinguished from those of
hadronic EOS variations and from nuclear-physics uncertainties.
Specifically, we address the following questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How do the gravitational-wave signal and the dynamical
        ejecta (mass, velocity, and thermodynamic conditions) of an
        equal-mass merger differ between hadronic EOSs and an EOS
        with a first-order hadron--quark phase transition, and are
        these differences larger than the numerical uncertainty?
  \item How do the EOS-induced changes in the nucleosynthesis yields,
        radioactive heating, and kilonova light curves compare with
        those produced by different theoretical prescriptions for
        $\beta$-decay rates, and does the joint GW+EM analysis help
        disentangle the two?
\end{enumerate}

The study is restricted to a single equal-mass binary, three EOSs
[names], and the dynamical ejecta; the long-term disk outflows and
neutrinos as a direct observable are beyond its scope.
